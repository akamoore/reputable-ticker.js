01 Section One · Summary

A quiet directional read.

Nothing moved strongly enough at the full-cohort level to clear the standard significance bar. Instead, the data lean — multiple sleep metrics drift in the same direction, three subgroup findings clear significance, and the overall picture matches what a mild fasting protocol would be expected to produce on wearable-detectable physiology.

The honest read of the cohort-level data: no single metric is strong enough on its own to anchor a claim. Total Sleep (p = 0.079) and REM Sleep (p = 0.098) sit in the trend zone — internally consistent, but not individually confirmable at this sample size (n = 44) in a 7-day window.

Where this study gets interesting is the subgroup layer. Three findings cleared significance: men fell asleep 18.8% faster (p = 0.028); the 26–35 age group gained 2.2 efficiency points (p = 0.033); moderate-stress participants lost 8% of their REM (p = 0.019). Three subgroup cells, three different metrics, three different directions — not the "one clean story" result. But each one makes physiological sense on its own.

What follows: the cohort who showed up, the full 19-metric breakdown, each of the subgroup findings in context, the individual-level distribution, the plausible mechanism, and six limitations that bound any reading.

An already-practicing wellness cohort. Of 67 baseline-survey respondents, 29 listed physical activity as a mental-health practice, 22 meditation, 16 breathing exercises, 12 yoga. Walking was reported by all 46 participants; strength training by 34. This is a group with existing sleep and metabolic routines — which narrows the expected room for additional intervention effects.

A younger-skewing composition. 26 of 46 participants (57%) are aged 18–45. That younger skew matters for two reasons: fasting physiology shifts with age (older adults tolerate fasting windows less well), and wearables detect sleep architecture more cleanly in participants with less fragmented baseline sleep — which younger cohorts tend to have.

The fasting protocol itself is unmeasured. The enrollment survey confirmed participants were willing to try a fasting reset. What fasting window they chose (16:8, 18:6, 24-hour, water-only), how many days they actually fasted, and when in their schedule they fasted are not in the dataset. The lack of protocol detail is the biggest interpretive constraint on this study.

03 Section Three · Results

The full picture.

19 metrics with paired data. Nothing cleared cohort-wide significance. What the chart shows instead: a cluster of sleep-related metrics trending downward together, cardiovascular markers essentially flat, and activity essentially unchanged — a coherent picture of mild sleep-volume reduction without any autonomic disruption.

What trended: Total Sleep (−2.6%, p = 0.079), REM Sleep (−3.8%, p = 0.098). Both sit in the "close to significance" zone. Neither individually meets the threshold for a strong claim, but their directional consistency is the interpretable part.

What stayed flat: HRV (+2.4%, n.s.), Average Heart Rate (−0.7%, n.s.), Sleep Efficiency (±0%), Sleep Latency (−5.1%, n.s.). Cardiovascular markers didn't move, sleep efficiency didn't move. That's a useful negative result — a "no autonomic disruption" signature that's worth naming alongside the total-sleep trend.

Sleep architecture split: REM drifted down, Light Sleep drifted down, Deep Sleep held roughly steady. If fasting mildly suppresses sleep, the metabolic-arousal hypothesis predicts REM is the stage most affected — and that's what the numbers show.

Activity held steady: Total Calories, Active Calories, Steps, Distance all changed by less than 1% with non-significant p-values. Participants appeared to maintain their normal physical activity during the intervention, which is notable for a protocol that explicitly restricts caloric intake.

Floors Climbed, VO₂ Max, and Stress Score came through as zeros across the Oura-dominant cohort — device doesn't report. Excluded from analysis.

04 Section Four · Subgroup signals

Three subgroup findings.

Where the cohort-level numbers are quiet, the subgroup slices produce three independently-significant findings. Each one sits on a small sample, so interpret them as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory — but each one has a physiological story that stands up.

Male participants fell asleep 18.8% faster.
Young adults gained 2.2 points of sleep efficiency.
Moderate-stress participants lost 8% of REM.
Three subgroups · three metrics · three different directions

Men, Sleep Latency

n = 11 · p = 0.028 · d = −0.77

The 11 male participants with sleep-latency data fell asleep on average 3.3 minutes faster during the intervention (17.6 → 14.3 min). Cohen's d of −0.77 is a large effect size — larger than any single-metric finding in the male subgroup across the program. Plausible mechanism: overnight fasting reduces late-evening glucose variability, which for some people eases sleep onset. The finding should be replicated with a larger male sample.

26–35, Sleep Efficiency

n = 10 · p = 0.033 · d = +0.80

The 10 youngest working-age adults gained 2.2 percentage points of sleep efficiency (87.8 → 90.0%) and saw Time Awake drop 23% (p = 0.084). For this cohort, the quieter overnight metabolic state appears to have translated into more consolidated sleep — despite a trend toward shorter total sleep. The pattern suggests "less sleep, but denser sleep" for this age group specifically.

Moderate-stress participants (n = 26) were the cohort's largest stress cell, and they carried the REM-sleep trend. REM dropped 8.0% in this subgroup (p = 0.019 Wilcoxon, d = −0.49). Plausible mechanism: fasting elevates ghrelin and cortisol awakenings modestly overnight, which preferentially suppresses REM. Moderate-stress participants may sit in a sweet spot where the metabolic change is detectable — low-stress participants are already low-arousal and have less room to move; high-stress participants have enough other variance that the fasting signal gets swamped.

The 46–55 age group (n = 9, not significant on its own) showed Sleep Efficiency drop 1.8% (p = 0.104) and Total Sleep drop 4.6% — directionally opposite to the younger cohort. Too small to draw a line from, but worth flagging: the same intervention may be net-positive for younger adults and net-neutral-or-negative for older ones. Consistent with the age-sensitive pattern we've seen in several other studies in the program.

The honest read across subgroups: fasting did something detectable, but what it did varied by who was doing it. No single subgroup result is large enough on its own to anchor a confident claim; the three together paint a picture that fasting's wearable-visible effects are modest, directionally-specific, and age-and-sex-modulated.

05 Section Five · Individual variation

Who slept less.

59% of participants slept less at night during the intervention. Among them, the median reduction was about 25 minutes. The non-responders were split — some gained modest amounts of sleep, a couple gained substantial amounts. The distribution matters more than the mean for an intervention this nuanced.

Per-participant change in Total Sleep
Each bar = one participant · sorted by magnitude · minutes per night

26 of 44 participants (59%) slept less at night during the intervention. Among this group, the median decline was 25 minutes per night; the bottom quintile lost more than an hour. For anyone in that bottom tail, the fasting protocol would have been a meaningful sleep cost — well beyond the cohort-level "about 11 minutes on average" framing.

18 participants (41%) slept more at night. The median gain was 15 minutes, with three participants gaining 60+ minutes. These gainers may have been people whose fasting schedule happened to quiet late-night eating patterns that had been fragmenting their sleep at baseline — a known mechanism for improved sleep with time-restricted eating.

The REM responder pattern ran parallel. 26 of 44 participants (59%) lost REM sleep; 18 gained. Among losers, several lost 15–30 minutes of REM — substantial, since total REM per night is usually in the 80–100 minute range. Anyone in the bottom tail of both Total Sleep and REM Sleep is the participant most likely to report "I felt more tired during the fast."

No cardiovascular tail. Lowest Sleep HR, HRV, and Average HR responders cluster tightly around zero — very few participants moved more than 5% in either direction on these metrics. That's another signal pointing to "fasting affects sleep volume but not autonomic state" at this duration.

06 Section Six · Mechanism

Why fasting touches sleep.

The sleep-volume signal in this study lines up with well-established fasting physiology. Three mechanisms, all small-to-moderate, combine to produce exactly the kind of mild sleep reduction the cohort averages show.

The hormonal window.

Fasting — especially extended or overnight-skewed fasts — shifts three hormone systems that the brain uses to manage sleep:

Ghrelin. The hunger hormone rises during fasting windows. Elevated ghrelin has a small but documented alerting effect — it's associated with increased nighttime wakings, particularly in the final third of the sleep cycle when REM sleep is dominant. The REM-specific sensitivity in the cohort-level data is consistent with this.

Cortisol. Fasting elevates baseline cortisol modestly, and cortisol has a well-mapped relationship with sleep depth and REM. Higher cortisol tends to preferentially suppress REM more than deep sleep — which matches the pattern in the data: REM down 3.8%, Deep Sleep essentially flat.

Adenosine. Adenosine (the sleep-pressure signal) accumulates from neuronal activity. Fasting doesn't change adenosine directly but can indirectly affect it via caffeine-intake changes — participants who stopped drinking coffee during fasting windows may have experienced either shorter latency (clearer adenosine buildup) or disrupted sleep (caffeine withdrawal). We can't separate this from the data.

What the pattern is consistent with: a mild metabolic-arousal effect that preferentially reduces REM, modestly shortens total sleep, but doesn't meaningfully shift cardiovascular state or daytime behavior. That's a measured, plausible read — not a claim that fasting is "bad for sleep," but a recognition that shifting the body's fuel-timing signals produces detectable downstream effects.

The 46–55 vs 26–35 split reinforces the picture. Younger metabolisms adapt to fasting faster; older metabolisms carry the arousal cost longer. Several weeks of adaptation would likely close this gap — but at 7 days, we see it.

07 Section Seven · Caveats

What we can't tell.

Six limitations that bound any reading of these results — and one that's especially important for a nutrition-timing study.

i.

No control group

Pre/post only. Without a matched-calorie-no-fasting control, we can't separate fasting's specific effect from general dietary change, seasonality, or the general effect of being in a study tracking your intake.

ii.

Fasting protocol unmeasured

The single most important caveat: we don't know which fasting protocol participants used. 16:8 intermittent fasting, 18:6 time-restricted eating, and 24-hour water fasts have markedly different metabolic and sleep profiles. Treating them together washes out protocol-specific effects.

iii.

Small sample

46 participants with full data; subgroup cells as small as n = 2. The three significant subgroup findings rest on n = 10–26 samples — hypothesis-generating at best. Replication at larger n is required before any of them should guide practice recommendations.

iv.

Compliance invisible

The dataset shows what wearables captured, not what participants ate (or didn't). How consistently the fasting window was maintained, how many calories were eaten during feeding windows, and whether anyone "broke" the fast are unknown. Responders and non-responders may differ in adherence rather than physiology.

v.

Short duration

Seven days captures the acute metabolic response to fasting but not the adaptation window that typically follows. Research on 2–4 week fasting protocols often shows sleep returning to baseline — the acute sleep-reduction signature here may diminish with sustained practice.

vi.

Cohort composition

70% female, 100% USA, 54% Caucasian, 37% above $100K, already-active-and-wellness-engaged at baseline. The cohort is both relatively young and relatively resourced — groups most likely to have favorable fasting outcomes. Generalization to less-engaged populations isn't supported.

08 Section Eight · Takeaway

The honest read.

This study produced no cohort-wide significant findings, a directional sleep-reduction trend (Total Sleep −2.6%, REM Sleep −3.8%), and three significant subgroup results. The data is consistent with mild fasting-induced sleep changes — not dramatic, not harmful, but directionally specific: REM-preferring, volume-reducing, cardiovascularly neutral.

What the data do support: a cautious claim that a 7-day fasting protocol produces small, detectable effects on sleep volume and REM sleep, with little impact on cardiovascular markers or daytime activity. The clearest positives are in the male subgroup (faster sleep onset) and the 26–35 age group (better sleep efficiency). The clearest cost is REM sleep in moderate-stress participants.

What the data do not support: broad claims about fasting improving or impairing recovery at the population level. The null cohort-level finding combined with the wide variance across subgroups means this study can't adjudicate the big fasting-for-health question.

What the data could do for programming: this is exactly the study that benefits most from adding protocol detail. If a future Fasting Reset logged which protocol each participant used (16:8 vs 18:6 vs 24-hour), the dataset would likely split into interpretable sub-studies where the protocol-specific effect becomes visible. That's the single highest-leverage change for the next version.

Recommended framing for communications: "Participants in this public study who tried a fasting reset showed a small trend toward shorter, lighter nights — about 11 fewer minutes of total sleep and a small drop in REM sleep on average. No metric cleared strict significance cohort-wide, but three subgroups showed meaningful effects: men fell asleep 18.8% faster (p = 0.028), younger adults (26–35) gained sleep efficiency (+2.5%, p = 0.033), and moderate-stress participants lost REM (−8%, p = 0.019). Cardiovascular markers and daytime activity stayed flat. The effects are consistent with known fasting physiology — mild metabolic arousal that preferentially touches REM — but the study can't isolate which specific fasting protocol produced which effect."
