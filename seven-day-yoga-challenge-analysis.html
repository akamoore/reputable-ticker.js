01 Section One · Summary

No clear effect.

With 19 measurable metrics and 49 participants, we'd expect roughly one "standout" finding just by chance. Instead, we got none — nothing moved enough at the whole-cohort level to clearly rise above normal day-to-day variation. That's the honest headline, and it's worth treating as informative rather than disappointing.

Across every sleep metric, every heart-rate metric, HRV, respiratory rate, steps, and calories — nothing shifted enough across the full 49-person group to clearly rise above everyday day-to-day variation.

This doesn't mean yoga "doesn't work." It means that seven days at this sample size wasn't enough to produce a signal that stood out from the noise. What follows is an honest look at where quieter signals appeared — and where they didn't.

Where the signals do appear, they concentrate in subgroups rather than the whole cohort: the moderate-stress HRV improvement (p = 0.029), a female-specific HRV increase (p = 0.036), and a reduction in total sleep and REM among women that cleared Wilcoxon significance (p = 0.022 and 0.041 respectively) — the latter in the opposite direction from what we might hope for.

Meanwhile, daytime activity increased slightly (steps +2%, distance +3%, medium-activity minutes +7%), which is intuitive — people were doing yoga. That's the opposite pattern from the Gratitude Effect and 30-Minute Reset studies, where daytime activity dropped during the intervention.

37% already practiced yoga (18 of 49 with insights data) — as a listed exercise activity or as a mental-health practice. For this subgroup, the "7-day yoga challenge" was less of a behavior change and more of an intensity bump.

Most were already active. 62 of 84 baseline-survey respondents said they walked regularly; 35 did strength training; 18 ran; 15 cycled. Exercise 3–5 days/week was typical. This is a cohort with an existing fitness floor, which may make additional short-term changes harder to detect.

Stress distribution is broader than the previous studies — 21 high-stress and 6 very-high-stress participants (vs 10 and 2 in the Reset, 13 and 3 in the Gratitude Effect). That's a stronger subgroup base for testing stress-mediated effects, had the overall signal been larger.

03 Section Three · Results

The full picture.

Effect sizes across all measurable metrics, sorted by magnitude. The story is quiet — nothing large, nothing statistically significant in the full cohort. Several metrics drift in unexpected directions.

Cardiovascular metrics all drifted upward — sleeping heart rate +2.3%, lowest sleeping heart rate +2.0%, average daytime heart rate +1.0%. None is significant, but the direction is worth noting. A week of additional physical activity plausibly nudges resting HR up rather than down, particularly for participants newly adopting practice.

Sleep signals were mixed — Time Awake trended down (-4.8%, p = 0.15 Wilcoxon), Deep Sleep nudged up (+2.5%, n.s.), but Total Sleep drifted down (-1.8%, p = 0.11 Wilcoxon), and Light and REM Sleep both declined modestly. It's a pattern of small movements in different directions, the statistical fingerprint of noise.

Activity went up (steps +2%, distance +3%, medium activity +7%) — intuitive for a yoga-added week and the opposite of the activity-decline pattern seen in the two stress-reduction studies.

VO₂ Max, Stress Score, and most Floors Climbed data came through as zeros — Oura Ring (the dominant device) doesn't report these in the dataset. Excluded from analysis.

04 Section Four · Where the signal lives

A quiet HRV signal.

The one finding that clears significance in a subgroup: moderate-stress participants saw a meaningful increase in heart rate variability. The effect is consistent with what yoga is supposed to do — but it's only visible if you look for it.

The positive reading

If we take the moderate-stress signal at face value

HRV is the closest proxy we have to parasympathetic tone on a wearable. A 10.7% rise in seven days, concentrated in people with moderate baseline stress, is consistent with what a yoga practice is supposed to deliver. That it reaches significance (p = 0.029) in a single subgroup is suggestive even if not definitive.

The cautious reading

Why to hold this loosely

With six stress subgroups examined across 19 metrics, one significant result at p = 0.029 is roughly what we'd expect from multiple testing alone. The low-stress group moving the opposite direction (−17%) and the female-specific finding combining an HRV rise with a reduction in REM sleep (p = 0.041) both suggest the picture is noisier than a single positive reading.

The other subgroup findings worth naming:

Female participants (n=40) showed an HRV increase of 7.6% (p = 0.036) — but also a Wilcoxon-significant reduction in Total Sleep (p = 0.022) and REM Sleep (p = 0.041). These mixed signals may reflect practice displacing sleep time rather than improving sleep quality.

The 56–65 age group (n=10) showed HRV improvement (Wilcoxon p = 0.020) and a reduction in REM sleep approaching significance (p = 0.061, Wilcoxon 0.065). Similar pattern to the female subgroup.

The 46–55 age group showed a borderline increase in average heart rate (+3.3%, p = 0.076) — trending the wrong direction for a calming practice.

No single finding in this section is large enough to anchor a claim. Together they suggest that yoga may produce real but small autonomic effects in seven days — and that you need more people, more time, or both, to see them clearly.

05 Section Five · Individual variation

Individual HRV change.

26 of 47 participants (55%) showed an HRV increase during the week — a bare majority. The story is one of small positive movements for most, a few larger gains, and a handful who went meaningfully backward.

Per-participant change in HRV
Each bar = one participant · sorted by magnitude · milliseconds

26 of 47 participants (55%) saw their HRV rise during the week. Among improvers, the median gain was +2.7 ms and the top five improvers gained 16–22 ms — genuinely large movements in HRV terms. Any single one of these cases, read in isolation, would look like a clear positive response.

19 of 47 (40%) saw HRV decline, including one outlier case with a 57-point drop — likely a device-measurement anomaly, but one real enough to shift the cohort mean. When the single largest outlier is removed, the mean HRV change goes from +0.9 to +2.1 ms, and the overall p-value drops accordingly — still not significant, but less null.

The HRV responder pattern is roughly 50/50, consistent with a small-but-real signal rather than a reliable intervention effect at this duration.

06 Section Six · Duration

Is seven days enough?

This is the shortest of the three public studies we've analyzed. It may also be the most important confound to name — if the effect exists but takes longer to show up, a short trial systematically underestimates it.

Statistical power matters here. With n = 49 and a 7-day measurement window, detecting a Cohen's d of 0.3 (the kind of effect we saw in the Reset study's sleep-latency finding) would require roughly 90 participants for 80% power. At n = 49, the study is only sensitive to d ≈ 0.4 or larger. Everything smaller than that is in the "can't distinguish from noise" zone.

Physiological adaptation to exercise is slow. HRV responses to exercise interventions typically take 4–8 weeks to stabilize in research literature. Cardiovascular remodeling from aerobic or flexibility training operates on month-scales, not week-scales. A 7-day intervention that showed large biometric shifts would actually be surprising.

What seven days can capture are acute, within-day changes — HRV the morning after a practice, heart rate during and immediately after sessions. What it can't capture are longer-term training adaptations, especially in people who were already active at baseline (as most of this cohort was).

The implication: a null 7-day result isn't evidence that yoga doesn't affect these metrics. It's evidence that 7 days at this sample size can't measure those effects clearly. A 4- or 8-week replication with the same cohort structure would be the next honest step.

07 Section Seven · Caveats

What we can't tell.

Six limitations that constrain what any reading of these results can claim.

i.

No control group

Pre/post design only. Any observed change could reflect seasonality, observation effects, or simply measurement noise rather than the yoga practice itself.

ii.

Short duration

Seven days is too short to expect physiological adaptation. The null result should be interpreted as "we couldn't detect an effect in this window" not as "there is no effect."

iii.

Compliance invisible

The data show what wearables captured, not what participants did. Whether they completed daily yoga sessions, for how long, and at what intensity is unknown.

iv.

Already-active cohort

37% already practiced yoga, and most were active 3–5 days/week at baseline. Adding one week of additional yoga to an already-active lifestyle produces smaller expected effects than starting from scratch.

v.

Subgroup multiplicity

With six stress subgroups × 19 metrics, chance alone should produce a few "significant" findings at p < 0.05. Treat subgroup results as hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory.

vi.

Sample composition

82% female, 98% USA, 71% Caucasian, 60% above $50K household income. The cohort is more age-diverse than the other studies, but not demographically representative.

The lesson across three studies: reflective and restorative practices produce detectable sleep-quality effects within weeks; seven days of added physical practice does not. The direction of the yoga signal (HRV up, activity up, heart rate up slightly) is consistent with an active intervention, not a calming one — which fits, since a yoga challenge adds activity rather than replacing it. What it doesn't do, at this duration and sample size, is produce the kind of large biometric shift we saw in the Gratitude Effect and 30-Minute Reset studies.

09 Section Nine · Takeaway

The honest read.

This study did not produce a statistically significant effect in any biometric metric at the full-cohort level. The main positive signal — an HRV increase of about 11% in moderate-stress participants — is consistent with what yoga should do physiologically, but is a subgroup result at borderline significance and shouldn't be overweighted.

Three plausible readings of the null:

Seven days isn't enough. Cardiovascular and autonomic adaptation to exercise takes 4–8 weeks. The study was measuring the wrong window to detect training effects.

The cohort was already active. 37% already did yoga, and most were exercising 3–5 days/week. There's less room to move when you start from a healthy baseline.

The intervention isn't cardiovascularly calming in the short term. Daily yoga adds movement. Heart rate metrics drifted slightly up (not down), consistent with more physical activity, not less. The intervention's effects on flexibility, strength, or subjective well-being aren't measured by wearables at all.

Recommended framing for communications: "Seven days of yoga didn't produce a statistically detectable change in cohort-wide biometrics, though a quiet HRV signal appeared in moderate-stress participants. The main interpretive challenge is duration: a week is too short to measure training adaptations. A replication at 4–8 weeks would be the honest next step before making claims about yoga's physiological effects in this population."
