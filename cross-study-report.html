The Cross-Study Report.

Eight wellness interventions tested across 324 participants using consumer wearables. 11 cohort-wide findings cleared statistical significance. What they reveal together is more interesting than any single study can show on its own — about sleep, about timing, about age, and about what this research design can actually detect.

02 Section Two · Ranked by effect

The eleven findings, in order.

Every cohort-wide p < 0.05 finding across the program, ranked by Cohen's d. Three stand out as unusually strong for consumer-wearable studies; the rest represent the small-to-moderate effects this research design is built to detect.

Rank Study Metric Δ n Cohen's d p (best)

The top three are all large-for-7-days effects. Sunrise Effect's step drop (d = −0.69, p = 0.0002) is the strongest single finding. The two Wim Hof findings (HRV down, sleep HR up, both d ≈ 0.63) come from a notably smaller sample (n=22), which means the underlying effect had to be large enough to clear significance at that sample size.

The middle tier clusters around d = 0.3–0.5. These are the findings the program is built to detect: small-to-moderate effects visible at 7 days with 30–50 participants. Sunrise Sleep Efficiency (+1.9%, d = 0.40), Gratitude Time Awake (−13%, d = −0.37), Reset Sleep Latency (−17%, d = −0.37). Meaningful effects at meaningful sample sizes.

The tail — Nature Dose REM and Time Awake, Hydration REM (d ≈ 0.28–0.34) — are closer to what the literature calls the "detectable floor" of this study format. Smaller than that, and a 7-day pre/post design mostly can't distinguish signal from noise.

03 Section Three · Reliable signals

Sleep is where the signal lives.

Of the 11 significant cohort-wide findings across 8 studies, 7 landed in sleep metrics — Time Awake (3 studies), REM Sleep (2), Sleep Efficiency (2), Sleep Latency (1). The remaining 4 split between activity (2) and cardiovascular (2, both in Wim Hof). The program's sensitivity map is clear.

Seven of eleven cohort-wide findings
landed in sleep metrics.
Time Awake, REM Sleep, Sleep Efficiency, Sleep Latency — the detectable space

What moves easily

Sleep metrics · cohort signals

Time Awake (restlessness in bed) and Sleep Efficiency (ratio of sleep time to time-in-bed) responded to five different intervention types — gratitude, circadian entrainment, nature exposure, and morning light. Both metrics measure sleep consolidation, and consolidation is what short behavioral interventions can meaningfully shift in 7 days.

What stays flat

Cardiovascular markers · 6 of 8 studies

HRV, average heart rate, and sleep heart rate moved meaningfully in only two studies: Wim Hof (activation, as designed) and the 30-Minute Reset (parasympathetic shift). Six other studies showed cardiovascular markers drifting less than 2%. The autonomic system doesn't typically retrain in a week — which means wearable studies at this duration are poorly positioned to detect "calming" effects via heart rate.

The implication for program design: treat sleep metrics as the primary outcome for any 7-day intervention study. Cardiovascular markers should be secondary — they're more valuable as a sanity check ("the intervention didn't stress the body") than as a success measure ("the intervention calmed the body"). The exception is any intervention designed to produce acute autonomic effects, where the cardiovascular signal is primary.

Total Sleep was notably not where signals landed. Five studies (Gratitude, Hydration, Power Nap, Nature, Wim Hof) showed small negative trends in total sleep, but none cleared significance at the cohort level. What changes in a week is how sleep is distributed, not how much of it happens overall.

04 Section Four · The activity paradox

Almost every intervention cost movement.

Six of eight studies showed daytime activity drifting down during the intervention — even ones you'd expect to add movement. Only two (Yoga, with small gains; Wim Hof, flat) didn't follow the pattern. This is a consistent measurement artifact worth understanding.

Daytime activity change across studies
% change in steps or active calories during intervention · below zero = activity dropped

Three plausible explanations, consistent with the cross-study pattern:

Time displacement. Any intervention that takes 15–30 minutes a day replaces something. For people whose displaced time would otherwise be spent walking or doing errands, this shows up as a measurable step drop. The Sunrise Effect's −12.9% steps (p = 0.0002) is the cleanest example — the observation was stationary, and it replaced morning walking for most participants.

Sedentary focus. Several of the interventions (Gratitude journaling, the Reset practice, Hydration tracking, Nature exposure, Sunrise watching) involve sitting or standing in place. Even when no particular activity is being displaced, the intervention itself doesn't add movement.

Wearable undercount during intervention. For ring-based wearables (Oura is the dominant device in this program, 60%+ of selections), time spent with hands occupied by another task — holding a cup of water, journaling, sitting in nature — produces slightly lower step counts than normal ambient wear.

The useful framing: interventions in this format should be expected to produce small activity drops, and those drops should be read as a side effect of doing the intervention, not as a health signal. The 30-Minute Reset's −13% active calories is consistent with "the practice itself isn't physically active" — the meaningful thing is what happened in the sleep data, not the step count.

05 Section Five · Timing vs Training

Timing beats training at seven days.

The clearest pattern across intervention types: practices that work by shifting timing (circadian, reflective, phase-anchoring) produced larger, more consistent signals than practices that work by producing training adaptation (physical exercise, cold exposure). This matches decades of adaptation physiology — and it has implications for what this program can and can't test well.

Timing interventions

Sunrise, Gratitude, Reset, Nature, Hydration (negative)

All five showed at least one significant cohort-wide finding within 7 days. Sunrise Effect alone produced three — the strongest cluster in the program. These interventions work by changing when the body receives signals (morning light, evening wind-down, daytime hydration) rather than by building new physiological capacity. Their effects are measurable quickly because the body's clock responds quickly.

Training interventions

Yoga (null), Wim Hof (activation only)

The two explicitly physical-training studies produced the most ambiguous cohort-wide signals. Yoga at 7 days had no full-cohort significance (only a moderate-stress HRV subgroup finding). Wim Hof showed strong signals — but they were activation signals, not training adaptations. HRV research consistently finds that cardiovascular and autonomic adaptations take 4–8 weeks to stabilize. Asking a 7-day study to detect training effects is asking for a miracle.

This pattern has a clear implication for program direction. The studies most likely to produce publishable, actionable findings under this design are ones that manipulate timing or state — light exposure, reflective practices, dietary timing, schedule shifts. Studies designed to test training interventions (strength, cardio, flexibility) should either be substantially longer (6–8 weeks minimum) or should measure acute within-session effects rather than baseline shifts.

The Wim Hof result is the edge case worth naming: it produced significant cohort-wide findings at 7 days, but those findings move in the activation direction rather than the usually-hoped-for calming direction. Training interventions that produce acute stress signatures (heart rate up, HRV down) can be detected quickly — just not in the way most wellness framing expects.

06 Section Six · Age stratification

Age stratification earns its keep.

Four of the eight studies produced subgroup-by-age findings cleaner than the cohort-wide results. In several, the age signal tells a more physiologically coherent story than the mean effect. The cross-study takeaway: these wellness interventions are often not universal — they work for specific physiology, against others.

Sunrise Effect is the clearest case. Cohort-level: Sleep Efficiency +1.9% (p = 0.006). Age 26–45: +3.9% to +4.7% efficiency, Time Awake dropped 23–31%. Age 56–65 (n = 5): Sleep Efficiency dropped 2.2%, REM down 26%. Same intervention, opposite direction, split cleanly by age. Morning light advances sleep phase — young/middle adults often have delayed rhythms and benefit; older adults already run early-phase and get harmed.

Hydration Effect showed a parallel age pattern: older participants (56–65 Sleep Latency +56.6%, p = 0.013; 46–55 Total Sleep −23 min, p = 0.005) were disproportionately affected by the sleep-disruption side effect. Nighttime bladder function is age-graded, and so was the intervention's cost.

The 30-Minute Reset concentrated its sleep-latency effect in high-stress participants (n = 6, 47% latency reduction). The Power Nap Challenge — null at cohort level — produced a clean 46–55 subgroup finding (Sleep Latency −26%, p = 0.032).

The cross-study lesson: these are not one-size-fits-all interventions. A report that says "this practice helps sleep" without stratifying by age understates the variability by design. Every intervention in this program had either a clear age-effect pattern or a clear stress-level pattern, and the subgroup findings were often the more actionable outputs than the cohort averages.

07 Section Seven · Cardiovascular flatness

Heart rate rarely moved.

Most interventions in this program were designed (implicitly or explicitly) as calming or restorative practices. If they worked as intended, cardiovascular markers should have shifted toward recovery. They mostly didn't. That says something about both the interventions and the measurement window.

Across eight studies, only two produced significant cardiovascular findings:

Wim Hof — HRV dropped 9.5% (p = 0.008), lowest sleep heart rate rose 1.5 bpm (p = 0.013). These move in the activation direction, consistent with the practice's sympathetic-engagement mechanism.

30-Minute Reset — Average heart rate dropped 4.5 bpm (p = 0.053, borderline). This moves in the calming direction.

Six studies — Gratitude, Yoga, Hydration, Power Nap, Nature Dose, Sunrise — produced no significant cardiovascular findings. HRV movements clustered within ±3%; heart rate movements within ±1 bpm. These are well inside the daily variability of these metrics, so "non-significant" is statistically appropriate.

Two possible reasons this matters. One: several of these interventions probably do have parasympathetic effects, just not ones large enough to show up at this sample size in 7 days. Two: wearable HRV is a noisy metric at the individual level, so detecting small cohort shifts requires either larger samples or longer measurement windows.

For program design, this is a useful constraint, not a weakness. The studies produced clear sleep-architecture findings where cardiovascular findings would have been nice-to-have. If future work wants to show autonomic effects, that may need either a dose-response design (stronger interventions) or a longer measurement window (4+ weeks) to overcome the HRV noise floor.

08 Section Eight · Lessons

Lessons for the next eight.

What eight studies have taught us about how to design the ninth through sixteenth. These are observations from the data — where the program's current design works well and where small changes would make future findings more robust, more actionable, and more defensible.

i.

Treat sleep as primary

Design studies to detect sleep-architecture or sleep-consolidation changes. Target metrics: Time Awake, Sleep Efficiency, REM Sleep, Sleep Latency. Cardiovascular markers should be secondary sanity-checks, not success measures.

Measurement priority
ii.

Right-size the sample

For Cohen's d ≈ 0.3 (the typical small-to-moderate effect in this domain), 80% power at 7 days requires roughly n = 90. At n = 30–50, the program is sensitive only to d ≈ 0.4+. Budget accordingly — smaller studies will mostly detect only the strongest interventions.

Power analysis
iii.

Match duration to mechanism

7 days is appropriate for timing/circadian and reflective interventions. Training adaptations (HRV improvement, aerobic fitness, cardiovascular remodeling) need 4–8 weeks. Don't ask a 7-day study to detect training effects it can't physiologically produce.

Duration by type
iv.

Stratify by age at design time

Age-effects are the most consistent subgroup pattern across the program. Instead of reporting them as secondary, over-sample specific age cells and design studies to detect them as primary outcomes. A 20-person 46–55 arm is often more informative than a 40-person mixed-age cohort.

Cohort design
v.

Measure compliance directly

Daily participant check-ins (did you do the practice today? for how long?) would separate responders from non-practicers. Currently, null or small results are confounded with compliance uncertainty. A two-line daily survey costs nothing and transforms interpretability.

Method upgrade
vi.

Add subjective outcomes

Wearables are blind to mood, perceived energy, subjective sleep quality, and cognitive performance. Several interventions in this program likely improved these without touching wearable metrics. A 5-question end-of-day survey closes the biggest measurement gap at minimal participant burden.

Outcome coverage
vii.

Use active controls where possible

Pre/post designs can't distinguish an intervention's specific effect from the general effect of being in a study. An "active control" arm (neutral journaling vs gratitude journaling; neutral audio vs breathwork) would let the program make attribution claims it currently can't.

Design rigor
viii.

Log intervention timing

The Hydration Effect was the clearest case: timing of water intake likely mattered more than volume, but wasn't captured. For any intervention where when matters (light exposure, fluid intake, meal timing, breathwork), capture timing as a first-class variable.

Critical covariate
09 Section Nine · Synthesis

The honest read.

Eight studies across 324 participants have produced 11 cohort-wide significant findings, most clustered in sleep metrics and most consistent with well-established chronobiological or behavioral mechanisms. The program reliably detects small-to-moderate effects (Cohen's d ≈ 0.3–0.7) in sleep and activity data at 7 days. It struggles with cardiovascular findings that would require either larger samples or longer windows.

What the program does well: runs rigorous, paired, within-person analyses on consumer wearables with consistent methodology; produces directionally interpretable findings within a week; surfaces age and stress subgroup patterns that individual studies couldn't reach on their own. The cross-study consistency is its real asset — the fact that 5 of 5 timing interventions produced sleep-consolidation findings is more persuasive than any single study.

What the program doesn't do: test training adaptations at appropriate duration, measure subjective outcomes, distinguish intervention effects from Hawthorne/study-participation effects, or capture compliance. These aren't criticisms — they're the consequence of an explicitly lightweight, fast-turnaround design that's valuable precisely because it's fast and lightweight.

What's next: the lessons in Section Eight are actionable. Right-sizing samples for target effect sizes, stratifying by age at design time, and adding 5-question subjective surveys would each individually improve future findings substantially. Moving toward active-control designs would transform what the program can claim from "this intervention produced effects consistent with X" to "this intervention caused Y." Both versions have value; the program is currently the first, and could be the second with modest design additions.

Where the program delivers its cleanest value: a publicly readable, wearable-measured demonstration that small behavioral interventions — particularly around sleep timing and light exposure — produce statistically detectable effects within a week in real-world cohorts. That's an uncommon contribution in a field that mostly publishes lab-condition studies or unmeasured self-report. The right framing isn't "these studies prove X" — it's "these studies show X is measurable at this scale, and here's the shape it takes."
