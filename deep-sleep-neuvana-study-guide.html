Deep Sleep: Neuvana Xen.

53 participants tried the Neuvana Xen vagus-nerve-stimulation device daily for six weeks. 36 delivered complete wearable data. Nine separate findings cleared statistical significance at the full-cohort level — the most of any study in the program to date — and the pattern is unusual: daytime activity rose, nighttime sleep tightened, and cardiovascular markers stayed mostly flat.

01 Section One · Summary

The opposite pattern.

Across a 42-day intervention — nearly seven times longer than the other studies in the program — participants showed daytime activity increases where most studies in the program have shown decreases. Sleep tightened modestly without changing in architecture. The signature is unusual for this research design, and the duration is likely why.

The activity-increase pattern is what makes this study distinctive. Across the program's 12 other studies, interventions have almost uniformly been associated with small activity decreases — participants who add a ritual (journaling, breathwork, reading, napping) tend to subtract some incidental daytime movement. This study shows the opposite. Activity rose across five metrics: steps (+10%), distance (+8.5%), active calories (+8%), medium-intensity minutes (+10%), total calories (+1.5%). Every one cleared significance.

The sleep pattern is more familiar — Time Awake and Total Sleep dropped together, with Sleep Efficiency holding steady. That's the "tighter but shorter" consolidation signature we've seen in several other studies. What's notable is that it co-occurred with the activity rise, rather than with the activity drop we've seen in most other sleep-targeting interventions.

What follows: the cohort, the 19-metric picture, the activity story, the sleep story, the subgroup breakdown (with one counterintuitive 46–55 finding), plausible mechanisms for the pattern, and six limitations worth naming.

Younger cohort than the program average. 26 of 36 participants (72%) are aged 18–45 — considerably younger-skewing than most other studies in this program, where 46–55 is often the dominant cell. This matters for interpretation: younger adults generally have more cardiovascular-adaptation capacity over a 6-week window, and they're more likely to increase baseline activity when attention is drawn to it.

Gender is more balanced. 22 women, 14 men (61%/39%). Most other studies in the program are 80%+ female. The better gender balance here lets us look at male-female subgroup differences more fairly — and some of them are notable (see Section Five).

Sponsored-study dropout. 53 initially enrolled in the 42-day protocol; 36 completed with full wearable data. A 32% dropout/incompleteness rate is higher than the program's 7-day studies, which typically hold above 85% completion. The longer duration and daily device commitment likely drove the attrition — but the finding sample is still adequate for the effect sizes observed.

03 Section Three · Results

The full picture.

19 metrics with paired data. Nine cleared cohort-wide significance — five activity, three sleep, one caloric. No cardiovascular metric cleared significance, though two trended.

What cleared significance: Steps (p = 0.0007), Distance (p = 0.003), Active Calories (p = 0.008), Medium Activity Minutes (p = 0.014), Total Calories (p = 0.017), Total Sleep (p = 0.028), Time Awake (p = 0.030), Light Sleep (p = 0.049). Nine findings — the largest cluster in any study in the program, and the first where the dominant direction is activity-up.

What trended: Average Heart Rate (+1.1%, p = 0.064 — direction up, likely from increased activity); Lowest Sleep HR (−0.6%, p = 0.080 — direction down, parasympathetic); Sleep Efficiency (+0.5%, p = 0.106). Three directional trends — a small parasympathetic cardiovascular signal underneath the more obvious activity story.

What stayed flat: HRV (+1.2%, n.s.), REM Sleep (−0.6%), Deep Sleep (+0.2%), Sleep Latency (−1.8%, n.s.), Sleeping Respiratory Rate. Sleep architecture didn't meaningfully restructure; HRV didn't shift. The effect is visible in the volume and timing of sleep, not its composition.

Floors Climbed, VO₂ Max, and Stress Score came through as mostly zeros across the Oura-dominant cohort — device doesn't report. Excluded from analysis.

04 Section Four · The activity signature

Movement went up.

Five activity metrics rose at significance. 75% of participants walked more. This is the inverse of the pattern seen in most other studies in the program, where sedentary interventions are accompanied by small activity declines. The question is how much of this is the device, and how much is the 42-day window.

Per-participant change in daily Steps

27 of 36 participants (75%) walked more during the intervention. Among this group, the median increase was 814 steps per day — a meaningful behavioral shift. Several participants gained 1,500+ steps/day, and two gained more than 3,000 steps/day. The distribution has a much longer right tail than left.

9 participants (25%) walked less. The median decrease was about 400 steps. No participant showed a dramatic activity collapse — the left tail is mild. This is consistent with a real positive effect plus small amounts of individual variability, rather than a regression to the mean or measurement artifact.

The other activity metrics tracked. Distance (+8.5%), Active Calories (+8%), and Medium Activity Minutes (+10%) all cleared significance and all showed 70%+ of participants moving in the positive direction. This isn't one noisy metric; it's a coherent behavioral shift across multiple independently-measured surfaces.

Total Calories rose +1.5%. That's the caloric consequence of the activity increase. The fact that it's modest rather than large suggests participants added lower-intensity movement (walking, light activity) more than they added vigorous training.

05 Section Five · Subgroup signals

Different patterns, different people.

With the better gender balance and more age-diverse cohort here than in other studies, subgroup patterns become visible. Men showed stronger sleep effects; women showed stronger activity effects. One age group went the opposite direction on sleep onset — worth naming.

Men · sleep signature

n = 14 · tighter, shorter nights

The 14 male participants drove most of the sleep-consolidation finding. Total Sleep dropped 2.3% (p = 0.020), Time Awake dropped 5.7% (p = 0.038), REM dropped 2.8% (p = 0.017). Directionally, men also showed Average Sleep HR and Lowest Sleep HR drop by about 1.1 bpm each at trend-level significance. The profile: shorter, more consolidated nights with a subtle parasympathetic tilt.

Women · activity signature

n = 22 · movement-up profile

The 22 female participants drove most of the activity-increase finding. Steps rose 11.1% (p = 0.005). Daytime heart rate rose 1.8% (p = 0.029), consistent with the movement increase rather than autonomic activation. Sleep metrics in women were flatter than in men. The profile: substantial behavioral activation during the day, less obvious sleep change at night.

The 46–55 counter-finding. Eight participants aged 46–55 showed Steps rise 13.7% (p = 0.019) — consistent with the cohort-level activity finding — but also showed Sleep Latency rise 16.3% (p = 0.015). That means participants in this age cell took about 2 minutes longer to fall asleep during the intervention. For a device marketed as a relaxation aid, this direction is unexpected and worth naming. With n = 8 the effect is too small to draw policy from, but the direction is clean and the p-value is low enough to note.

The 18–25 cardiovascular signal. Four participants in the youngest age cell showed HRV rise 12.7% (p < 0.001 at that n) and Lowest Sleep HR drop 3.2% (p = 0.007). Both direction and magnitude are consistent with a parasympathetic training effect — exactly what you'd hope a vagus-nerve-stimulation device would produce. But n = 4 is too small to generalize. Replicate this subgroup specifically if the program continues in this direction.

The 26–35 cohort (n = 12) showed Steps rise 13.8% (p = 0.048) and trend-level Time Awake reduction (−6.9%, p = 0.085). Similar direction to the cohort-level finding, with the sample better-powered than 46–55 or 18–25.

A note on subgroup variance. The split between men (sleep-signal) and women (activity-signal) is unusually clean. It could reflect a genuine physiological difference in how men and women responded to the device over 42 days — or it could be a compositional artifact (the women in this cohort were younger on average, and younger adults often move more when prompted). We can't isolate these from the data we have.

06 Section Six · Mechanism

What 42 days can do.

The activity-up signature is the element of this study hardest to attribute cleanly. Three mechanisms plausibly contribute, and without a control arm we can't separate them. Naming each one matters for how the finding gets communicated.

Three plausible drivers of the activity increase.

One · Vagal-tone training. Vagus nerve stimulation, performed daily, can over time increase parasympathetic tone — which in turn supports faster cardiovascular recovery between activity bouts. Participants whose recovery capacity improves over 42 days may simply be able to do more daytime activity without feeling wiped out. This is the most charitable mechanism for attributing the activity increase to the device itself. The subtle overnight-heart-rate trend (Lowest Sleep HR −0.6%, p = 0.08) is consistent with this direction at trend level.

Two · The six-week attention effect. Participants who commit to a daily practice for 42 days have six weeks of implicit "be healthier" prompting. This is a well-documented effect in wellness research — any long-duration intervention that requires daily engagement produces behavioral halo effects on adjacent habits (nutrition, exercise, sleep hygiene) independent of the intervention's specific mechanism. A 7-day version of this study would likely show a much smaller activity increase.

Three · Seasonality over a 6-week window. 42 days is long enough for seasonal behavior shifts to partially contaminate the baseline-vs-intervention comparison. If the baseline week captured lower-activity weather and the intervention window captured higher-activity weather, we'd see activity increases from weather alone. The activity metrics most affected — Steps, Distance, Medium Activity Minutes — are exactly the ones most seasonally-sensitive.

What the data probably contains: some of each. The 75% responder rate on steps is too high for pure seasonality (which would bias toward noisy individual responses), and the coherence across multiple activity metrics is too clean for pure attention effects (which would be messier). A real device-related signal probably exists, embedded in behavioral and seasonal context that the design can't fully separate.

The most defensible claim: after 42 days of daily Neuvana Xen use, this cohort of 36 participants walked meaningfully more, spent less time awake at night, and slept slightly shorter, more consolidated nights. Cardiovascular markers shifted in small-but-directionally-consistent ways. A randomized-controlled version of this study would be required to separate the device-specific effect from the 6-week-engagement effect — but the signal in this dataset is large enough that it would be worth running.

07 Section Seven · Caveats

What we can't tell.

Six limitations worth naming — two of them unique to this 42-day design.

i.

No control group

Pre/post only. Without a matched-attention control (e.g., a sham-stimulation or audio-only group), we can't separate the device's specific effect from the six-week engagement effect, seasonality, or regression to the mean.

ii.

42 days brings seasonal variance

Six weeks is long enough for ambient weather, daylight hours, holiday schedules, and life-rhythm shifts to partially contaminate the baseline-vs-intervention comparison. This affects activity metrics most directly. Shorter studies don't carry this confound; this one does.

iii.

Compliance unmeasured

The dataset shows wearable metrics and surveys, not how often participants actually used the device or for how long per session. "Daily use" may reflect 5–7 sessions per week for some and 1–3 for others. Responders and non-responders may differ in practice dose, not biology.

iv.

Attrition is real

53 enrolled, 36 completed with full wearable data — about a third dropped or had incomplete data. Longer studies always lose more participants than short ones, and the completers may be systematically different (more motivated, more wellness-engaged) from the original enrollment.

v.

Cohort composition

36 completers, 100% USA, 61% female, 56% Caucasian, 42% above $100K income, younger than the program average. The result is cleanest in the 22-person female and 12-person 26–35 subgroups — which matches composition but narrows generalization.

vi.

Survey data unanalyzed here

The 3 survey check-ins (baseline, day 21, day 42) capture subjective sleep quality, daytime fatigue, and stress — outcomes that wearables can't measure. This analysis covers the biometric half only. A full picture of the intervention requires reading the survey changes alongside the wearable signals.

08 Section Eight · Takeaway

The honest read.

This study produced nine cohort-wide significant findings — tied for the most in the program's history. The dominant pattern is unusual for the design: activity rose across five metrics, with 75% of participants walking more during the intervention. Sleep tightened modestly (less time awake, slightly shorter nights). Cardiovascular markers shifted in directionally-consistent but mostly trend-level ways.

What the data do support: a claim that 6 weeks of daily Neuvana Xen use was associated with a meaningful, multi-metric increase in daytime activity and a small sleep-consolidation effect. The direction and coherence of the findings are strong enough that something real is happening — the question is how much of that something is the device versus the engagement.

What the data do not support: a causal claim that vagus nerve stimulation specifically produced these effects. The 42-day engagement window, combined with a single-arm design and no compliance logging, means the study can't separate the device's action from the "any 6-week daily practice raises activity" effect that's well-documented in wellness research. A randomized controlled version is the next honest step.

What the data could do for programming: this is the first activity-up study in the program, which makes it a natural pairing for an RCT design. If a 42-day Neuvana-vs-sham trial replicated the activity increase while eliminating the engagement confound, the finding would move from "interesting pattern" to "attributable effect."

Recommended framing for communications: "Over 42 days of daily Neuvana Xen use, 36 participants showed significant increases in daytime activity — about 646 more steps per day on average (p = 0.0007), along with increases in distance, active calories, and medium-intensity minutes. Nighttime sleep tightened modestly — time awake in bed dropped about 5% (p = 0.030), and total sleep dropped slightly. Cardiovascular markers shifted in small, directionally-consistent ways at trend level. Without a control group, we can't fully separate the device's specific effect from the engagement effect of a 6-week daily practice — but the signal direction and consistency are strong enough to merit a controlled replication."
